AI execution, delegation and operating cadence

AI delegation is assigning a bounded task to a software system while keeping a person accountable for the result. An executable brief states the outcome, permitted inputs, constraints and acceptance evidence. Review effort and the consequences of an incorrect output determine whether a task belongs in an AI workflow.

Quick answer

Begin with repeatable work whose result a person can inspect; keep permissions narrow and retain approval for consequential actions.

Frequently asked questions

What makes a task suitable for AI delegation?

Describe the task rules, how often inputs change, how a wrong output would be noticed and who bears its consequences. Prefer a bounded draft or classification task with an inspectable result. Do not assume that a fluent answer demonstrates factual accuracy, permission to act or suitability for an irreversible decision.

What should I put in an AI brief?

State the desired artifact, allowed information, prohibited actions, deadline and definition of done. Name who reviews the output and what evidence they need to accept it. Include a stop condition for missing information so the system can report uncertainty instead of filling gaps with plausible details.

Where does AI belong in an operating review?

Use it to assemble inputs, identify missing fields and draft a follow-up list that an owner checks. Keep responsibility for priorities, sensitive judgments and commitments with people. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework is a source for organizing governance questions; it does not certify an individual tool or workflow.

Where can I check the underlying requirements?

  • National Institute of Standards and Technology: AI Risk Management Framework — A published, non-commercial framework for governing AI use, instead of a vendor's own maturity model.
  • National Institute of Standards and Technology: AI RMF Development — How the framework was developed, for readers checking whether it is a real standard.
