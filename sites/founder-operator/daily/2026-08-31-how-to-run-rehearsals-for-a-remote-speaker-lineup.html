How to Run Rehearsals for a Remote Speaker Lineup

Short answer: Split rehearsal into three stages rather than one long session: an individual tech check per speaker, a content run for timing and slides, and a transitions rehearsal with the production team. Combining them wastes everyone's time, because a tech problem in the first ten minutes destroys a session that eight people cleared their calendars for.

What this page recommends

Split rehearsal into three stages rather than one long session: an individual tech check per speaker, a content run for timing and slides, and a transitions rehearsal with the production team.

Why one big rehearsal fails

The default is to book one rehearsal and invite everybody. It reliably goes the same way: the first speaker has a microphone problem, twenty minutes disappear, and the session ends having tested nothing that mattered while eight people watched.

Staging fixes this. Tech problems get solved one-to-one, cheaply, before anyone else is in the room. By the time the group session happens, the technical baseline is known and the time can go to the thing that actually needs the group: the seams between segments.

Three rehearsal stages, who attends, and what each one is for
StageWho attendsRough durationWhat it must confirm
Individual tech checkOne speaker plus one producer15 to 20 minutesCamera, microphone, lighting, connection, screen share, and the exact buttons they will use
Content runSpeaker plus producer, or a small groupLength of the segment plus 15 minutesTiming, slide behaviour, embedded media, and text legibility on a stream
Transitions rehearsalProduction team, speakers for entry and exit only60 to 90 minutesEvery switch, cue, and fallback in the run of show
Platform dry runProduction team only30 minutesPermissions, backstage flow, recording, captioning, and chat controls
Day-of checkEveryone, staggered10 minutes per speakerNothing changed since rehearsal on the speaker's own setup

What to confirm in an individual tech check

Keep it to fifteen minutes by knowing exactly what you are testing before you join.

  • Camera framing, with the speaker seated as they will actually sit.
  • Microphone source, and confirmation that no other device is competing for it.
  • Lighting, especially windows behind the speaker.
  • Connection stability, tested from the location they will present from.
  • Screen share, including a second monitor if they intend to use one.
  • Any embedded video or audio in their materials, played through the platform.
  • The exact join link, the join time, and where they wait beforehand.
  • Who they contact if something fails five minutes before they go live.

What each stage tests

The tech check is short and individual. Camera, microphone, lighting, connection, screen share, and the specific mechanics of the platform being used. It should end with the speaker knowing exactly which button they will press and what they will see when they do.

The content run is about timing and materials. Speakers reliably run long, and finding that out during rehearsal is much cheaper than finding out live. This is also where slide problems appear: fonts that do not travel, videos embedded in decks, and text sized for a room rather than a stream.

The transitions rehearsal is for the production team, with speakers present only for their entry and exit. This is the stage that determines whether the show looks professional, and it is the one most often skipped because it feels like it is not about the content.

Handling the speaker who will not rehearse

Some speakers will not attend a rehearsal. Rather than treating this as a discipline problem, treat it as a risk to be designed around: put them in a slot where a technical failure is recoverable, brief their assistant, and prepare a fallback that does not depend on them.

Record a short walkthrough video that covers the same ground as the tech check so there is a self-serve option. It will not fully substitute, but it converts some no-shows into partially prepared speakers.

Where the risk is genuinely unacceptable, pre-record the segment. A pre-recorded contribution from a speaker who cannot rehearse is almost always better than a live one.

Frequently asked questions

How far ahead should tech checks happen?

Far enough ahead that a discovered problem can be fixed, which usually means at least a week for anything requiring new equipment or an IT change. A tech check the day before is better than none, but it converts problems into emergencies rather than into tasks.

Do speakers need to rehearse from the same location they will present from?

Yes, wherever possible. Connection quality, background noise, and lighting are properties of the location rather than the person. A rehearsal from a quiet home office tells you very little about a presentation delivered from an office with a glass wall behind it.

What if a speaker refuses to rehearse?

Design around it rather than escalating. Place them where a failure is recoverable, brief whoever supports them, prepare a fallback, and offer a recorded walkthrough as a self-serve alternative. If the risk is unacceptable, pre-record the segment instead.

Is the transitions rehearsal really necessary?

It is the one that most affects how the show looks. Content is usually the part speakers have practised; the seams are the part nobody has. Skipping it saves an hour of preparation and spends it back on the day, in front of an audience.
