How to Tell a Performance Problem From a Role Design Problem

Short answer: Ask whether a strong performer with the same brief, the same authority, and the same constraints would succeed. If the honest answer is no, the problem is role design, not the person. Role design failures show up as repeated misses across different people, blocked decisions, and outcomes that depend on authority the role does not have.

Updated 2026-08-27. Topic cluster: hiring standards for small teams. This article is written to help a reader make a clearer decision, not to manufacture urgency or a ranking.

What this page recommends

Ask whether a strong performer with the same brief, the same authority, and the same constraints would succeed.

The substitution test

The single most useful question is a substitution: if you replaced this person with the strongest operator you know, holding the brief, the authority, and the constraints constant, would the outcome change? Managers often answer that question honestly and immediately, and are surprised by their own answer.

If the outcome would not change, the role is the problem. Common causes are an outcome that depends on a decision the role cannot make, a brief that quietly contains two jobs, a capacity mismatch where the work exceeds the hours available, or a dependency on another team with no shared priority.

Before the difficult conversation

Work through these in order. Each one you skip is a claim you will have to defend later.

  1. Re-read the scorecard and confirm the outcome being missed is actually written in it.
  2. Run the substitution test honestly, out loud, with one other person.
  3. Check whether previous holders of the role struggled in the same way.
  4. Verify the person has the decision rights the outcome requires.
  5. Compare the brief against a real week of their calendar and workload.
  6. Test the specific blocker they have named, rather than assessing whether it sounds convincing.
  7. Confirm the standard was communicated before the miss, not after it.
  8. Follow your organization's documented process, and confirm it with HR or counsel before acting.

Patterns that point at design rather than performance

Repetition across people is the strongest signal. If the last two holders of the role struggled in similar ways, the odds that you hired badly twice in the same shape are lower than the odds that the role is shaped wrong.

Another signal is when the person's assessment of what is blocking them has been consistent and specific for weeks, and nobody has tested it. Vague complaints are weak evidence; a specific, repeated, checkable claim that has never been checked is a management gap.

A third is when success requires influence rather than authority, and nobody said so. Some roles are legitimately influence-based, but that has to be an explicit design choice, staffed by someone who was hired for it and told about it.

What to do with each diagnosis

If it is role design, change the design before changing the person: narrow the outcomes, grant the missing decision right, or split the role. Then set a fresh review window so the change gets a fair test rather than being judged against the old expectations.

If it is genuinely performance, be direct and specific about which outcome is being missed and what a clear improvement would look like, and follow whatever documented process your organization and jurisdiction require. Ambiguity here is unkind to the person and creates legal exposure for the organization.

If it is context, such as a reorganization or a shifted priority that made the role's outcomes irrelevant, say that plainly. Letting someone believe they failed at a job that was quietly cancelled is one of the more corrosive things a manager can do.

Frequently asked questions

What if it is both a performance problem and a design problem?

That is common. Fix the design first anyway, because a fixed design gives you a clean read on the performance question. Assessing someone against a role that was impossible produces a conclusion you cannot rely on and cannot defend.

How long should a redesigned role get before you reassess?

Long enough for one full cycle of the work to complete. For most operational roles that is 3 months; for roles with long feedback loops it is longer. Setting the window in advance, in writing, protects both sides from a moving target.

Does splitting a role always solve an overload problem?

No. Splitting adds a coordination cost and creates a new seam where work can fall through. It helps when the two halves genuinely have different outcomes and different skills. It makes things worse when the real problem was an unclear priority, because now two people are unclear instead of one.

Who should be involved in this diagnosis?

At minimum, one person outside the reporting line who can push back on the manager's account. Where the outcome may affect someone's employment, involve HR or a qualified employment attorney early, because process requirements differ by jurisdiction and are difficult to repair retroactively.
