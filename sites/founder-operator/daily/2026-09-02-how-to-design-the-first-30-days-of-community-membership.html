How to Design the First 30 Days of Community Membership

Short answer: Design the first 30 days as three moments: the first ten minutes, where a new member needs to see somebody like them; the first week, where they need one successful interaction; and the first month, where they need a reason to have a habit. Communities usually invest in acquisition and leave all three to chance.

What this page recommends

Design the first 30 days as three moments: the first ten minutes, where a new member needs to see somebody like them; the first week, where they need one successful interaction; and the first month, where they need a reason to have a habit.

The first ten minutes

A new member arriving in an unfamiliar space is asking one question: are these my people. They answer it by scanning, not by reading. What they need in the first ten minutes is evidence that someone with their problem is here and is being helped.

This is why a wall of announcements is a poor landing experience and a visible recent thread where someone got a useful answer is a good one. It is also why a welcome message that describes the community's mission does less work than one that points at three current conversations.

Set these up before you invite anyone

Most of these take an afternoon and are almost never done in advance.

  • A landing view that shows recent useful conversation rather than announcements.
  • A first prompt that can be answered honestly in one line.
  • A named person or rota responsible for answering every first post within a day.
  • A way to route a new member to the conversation that matches their stated problem.
  • At least one recurring anchor scheduled for the next three months.
  • A written note of what data you collect at signup and what you will do with it.
  • A plan for what to say to someone who goes quiet, sent by a person.
  • A definition of what a healthy first month looks like, agreed before launch.

The first successful interaction

The strongest predictor of a member returning is having said something and had someone respond. That means the first week's design problem is not content, it is making sure that a new member's first post does not sit unanswered.

Communities solve this with a small commitment rather than a clever mechanic: a named person, or a rota, responsible for making sure no introduction and no first question goes unanswered for a day. It is unglamorous and it is most of the work.

Lowering the cost of the first post also helps. A prompt that can be answered in one line gets far more first posts than an open invitation to introduce yourself, which quietly asks a newcomer to write a biography in front of strangers.

Turning a visit into a habit

By the end of the first month, the member either has a reason to come back on a schedule or they do not. Recurring anchors help: a weekly thread, a monthly call, a predictable rhythm they can plan around.

Watch for the quiet member who reads and never posts. They are not a failure state, and they are also invisible to most engagement metrics, which is a reason to be careful about optimizing for posts. Ask what they came for before assuming they need to be activated.

Finally, decide what happens when someone stops appearing. A single, human, non-automated note asking whether the community turned out to be useful will produce more honest information about the programme than a survey.

Frequently asked questions

Should new member onboarding be automated?

The routing and reminders can be. The first response to a person's first post should not be, because the entire value of that moment is that a human noticed. An automated welcome followed by silence is worse than no welcome, since it demonstrates that the space is running on autopilot.

What is a reasonable target for first-post response time?

Within a day is a workable commitment for most communities and is achievable with a small rota. What matters more than the exact target is that the commitment is explicit and someone owns it, because the failure mode is not slowness but an unanswered post that everyone assumed someone else would take.

Are lurkers a problem to be fixed?

Not necessarily. Many members join to read, and they may be getting exactly what they came for. The mistake is optimizing only for posting activity and then concluding a community is failing. Ask a sample of quiet members what they came for before designing interventions aimed at them.

How do we know whether the first 30 days is working?

Pick one observable measure before launch, such as the share of new members who post at least once and return in the following week. It will be imperfect, and it is still better than assessing the programme by how the space feels to the person who runs it.
