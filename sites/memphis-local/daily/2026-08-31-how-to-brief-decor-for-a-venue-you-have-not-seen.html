How to Brief Decor for a Venue You Have Not Seen

Short answer: Photographs hide the four things that matter most: ceiling height, where the power is, what the lighting actually does, and where people will stand. Get those in numbers and in writing from the venue, take a video walkthrough rather than stills, and ask your vendor what they would check if they were standing in the room.

What venue photographs are for

Venue photographs exist to sell the venue. They are taken at the best time of day, from the best angle, with the room empty and styled by somebody else. That is not dishonest, but it means they are close to useless as a planning document.

The specific things they conceal are consistent: how high the ceiling really is, where the power outlets are, what the light does at the actual hour of your event, and how the room feels once a hundred people and their chairs are in it.

Ask the venue for these in writing

Everything here is a fact the venue already holds. Getting it in writing also creates a record if the room differs.

  • Ceiling height at the lowest and highest points of the usable area.
  • Room dimensions, and a floor plan if one exists.
  • Outlet locations and available power capacity.
  • Doorway widths and the route from the loading area to the room.
  • What the lighting does and who controls it during the event.
  • What is fixed in the room and cannot be moved or covered.
  • A video walkthrough taken at the time of day your event runs.
  • A photograph from a previous event at a similar guest count.

The remote survey

Ask the venue for numbers rather than impressions. Ceiling height, room dimensions, doorway width, and outlet locations are facts the venue holds and will usually provide on request. A floor plan, if one exists, answers most of this at once.

Ask for a video walkthrough taken at the time of day your event runs, ideally by someone who will narrate. A slow pan across a room, with someone standing in it for scale, contains more usable information than twenty photographs.

Ask what the lighting does. Whether it dims, whether it is warm or cold, whether anything is on a timer, and whether the venue controls it or you do. Decor designed for one lighting environment can look completely different in another, and this is the variable most often discovered on the day.

Designing with a margin

When briefing remotely, ask the vendor to design with tolerance: pieces that work across a range of dimensions rather than one that fits exactly. The cost of that flexibility is usually small, and it converts a potential failure into a minor adjustment.

Agree what happens if the room is not as described. Who absorbs the cost of a change on install day, and what is the fallback design. Having that conversation while the design is still on paper is far easier than having it in a loading bay.

If the budget allows a site visit by the vendor before install day, it is nearly always worth it for a large installation. If it does not, ask the vendor to write down the three assumptions they are most worried about, and check each one with the venue.

Frequently asked questions

Is a video walkthrough really better than photographs?

Considerably, especially when someone narrates it and stands in the room for scale. Continuous footage shows how spaces connect, where the light falls, and what is in the corners, all of which still photographs are selected to exclude.

What is the single most important measurement to get?

Ceiling height, because it constrains everything vertical and is the dimension photographs distort most. Doorway width comes second, since it determines whether anything built off site can physically enter the room.

Should the vendor visit the venue before install day?

For a large installation it is usually worth the cost, because a single visit of 60 minutes removes most of the assumptions. When a visit is not possible, ask the vendor to name the three assumptions they are least confident about and verify each one with the venue in writing.

What if the room turns out to be different on the day?

Agree in advance who absorbs the cost of an on-the-day change and what the fallback design is. Designing with tolerance rather than to exact dimensions costs little and turns a potential failure into an adjustment made in the loading bay rather than a crisis.
