How to Compare Two Event Decor Quotes That Look Different

Short answer: Normalise before you compare. Rewrite both quotes onto the same line items: design, materials, setup labour, takedown, rental versus purchase, travel, contingency, and taxes or fees. The cheaper quote is very often the one that excluded takedown, assumed you own the table, or priced a shorter service window.

Two quotes are almost never the same scope

When two decor quotes differ substantially, the usual explanation is not that one provider is expensive. It is that they quoted different jobs. One included takedown and one did not. One assumed the venue supplies tables. One priced a three-hour service window and one priced six.

This is not usually deception. Providers quote against the brief they received and their own default assumptions, and if the brief left something open, each of them filled it differently. The fix is on your side: send an identical brief, then normalise what comes back.

Send both providers the same brief

Comparability starts with the brief, not with the quote. Send both providers the same eight points and the same deadline, allowing at least 5 days for a reply.

  1. The venue, with its access rules and load-in window.
  2. The date, the guest count, and the hours the setup must hold up.
  3. Whether you want pieces to be rented or purchased.
  4. Whether takedown is required and at what time.
  5. What the venue supplies, such as tables, linens, or power.
  6. A request that exclusions be listed explicitly.
  7. A request for change and cancellation terms in writing.
  8. A request that the total state whether taxes and fees are included.

The line items that hide the difference

Takedown is the most commonly excluded item, and it is real labour, often at an antisocial hour. Ask explicitly whether it is included and when it happens.

Rental versus purchase is the second. A quote that includes purchased pieces you keep is not comparable to one where everything returns with the vendor, even though both produce the same room on the night.

Travel, parking, and load-in labour are the third group, particularly where a venue has a difficult loading route or charges for dock access. These appear as surcharges on one quote and are absorbed into the total on another.

Finally, look for contingency and change terms. What happens if the guest count moves, if the venue changes the load-in window, or if the event is postponed. A quote with clear terms is worth more than a slightly cheaper one without them.

How to run the comparison

Build a single table with one row per line item and one column per provider, and fill in every cell, including the ones where the answer is not stated. Empty cells are the finding: they are the questions to go back with.

Ask both providers the same follow-up questions and note who answers specifically. Willingness to write down exclusions is a reasonable proxy for how the working relationship will go.

Then compare on total delivered scope rather than on the headline number. If one provider is more expensive and the difference is entirely takedown and a longer service window, they may simply have quoted the job you actually need.

Frequently asked questions

Why is one quote so much cheaper?

Most often because it covers less: no takedown, a shorter service window, a smaller crew, or an allowance rather than a fixed materials cost. Rewrite both onto the same line items before concluding anything about value. Occasionally the difference is real, and normalising is how you find out.

Is it reasonable to show one provider another's quote?

Sharing the scope is reasonable and useful; sharing a competitor's pricing is generally not, and it damages the working relationship. Ask instead whether they can quote against the same explicit scope, which gets you a comparable number without putting anyone in an awkward position.

What should I do about empty cells in the comparison?

Treat them as your question list. An unstated line item is the most likely source of a later surprise, so go back to both providers with the same questions and fill every cell before deciding. This step usually takes one email and prevents the most common invoice disputes.

Does the cheapest quote ever win outright?

Sometimes, once both are normalised to the same scope. What should not decide it is a headline number compared against a different job. Compare delivered scope, terms, and the specificity of the answers you got, and only then look at which total is lower.
