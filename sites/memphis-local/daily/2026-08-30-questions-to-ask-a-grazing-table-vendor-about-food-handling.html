Questions to Ask a Grazing Table Vendor About Food Handling

Short answer: Ask five things: whether the provider holds the food-service permits your local health department requires, how food is kept at temperature in transport and on the table, who monitors it during the event, how allergens are separated and labelled, and what happens to items at the end of the service window. A provider who does this professionally will answer all five specifically.

Why hosts do not ask, and why they should

Food handling is the least glamorous part of booking a grazing table and it is almost never discussed, partly because hosts assume it is handled and partly because it feels rude to ask. It is not rude. A provider who does this professionally is used to the questions and will answer them without hesitation.

The reason it matters is that a grazing table sits out, at room temperature, on a shared surface, for a defined period, often at a venue with no kitchen. That is a genuinely more demanding situation than plated service, and the difference between providers who have thought about it and those who have not is not visible in photographs.

Before you sign anything

Ask for these in writing. A provider who does this professionally will not find the request unusual.

  • Confirmation of the permits the provider holds and where food is prepared.
  • A description of how cold items are transported and held.
  • Whether the provider remains on site during the service window.
  • The stated length of the service window and what happens at the end of it.
  • How allergens are separated in preparation and on the table.
  • Whether items will be labelled, and how.
  • Confirmation of liability insurance.
  • Written confirmation, at least 7 days before the event, that the agreed window and menu still stand.
  • What changes if the event moves outdoors or the timing shifts.

The five areas worth asking about

Permits and licensing come first. Requirements for preparing and serving food commercially are set locally, and your local health department is the body that defines what applies. Ask the provider what they hold and where they prepare. A provider preparing in a licensed kitchen will say so.

Transport and holding is second. Food travels to the venue, waits, and then sits on the table. Ask how it is kept at temperature at each stage and what equipment is used, particularly for anything that needs to stay cold.

Monitoring during the event is third, and it is the one most often unanswered. Ask who is responsible for watching the table during the service window and whether they remain on site. Some providers style and leave; some stay and restyle. Both are legitimate, and you should know which you are buying.

Allergen handling is fourth. Ask how items are separated during preparation and on the table, whether anything is labelled, and what the provider can honestly promise. Guests with serious allergies deserve an accurate answer rather than a reassuring one.

Fifth is the end of the window. Ask what happens to remaining food, whether anything is packed for guests, and who takes responsibility for that decision. This is a question worth asking before the event, because it is often decided in a hurry afterwards.

What a good answer sounds like

Specificity is the signal. A provider who names where they prepare, describes their transport equipment, and states plainly whether they remain on site is telling you they have a procedure. A provider who says everything will be fine has told you nothing you can act on.

It is also reasonable to ask for these answers in writing, particularly the allergen ones. Not because you expect to need them, but because writing an answer down is how a provider checks that it is actually true.

Frequently asked questions

Is it reasonable to ask a vendor about permits?

Yes, and professional providers expect it. Requirements for commercial food preparation and service are set locally, so the specifics vary; your local health department is the authority on what applies in your area. A provider who is uncomfortable with the question has told you something useful.

How long can food safely sit out?

This guide deliberately does not publish a figure, because holding times depend on the food, the temperature, and rules set by food-safety authorities that vary by jurisdiction. Ask your provider what limit they work to and how they monitor it, and check your local health department's guidance if you want the underlying requirement.

Should the vendor stay for the whole event?

Not necessarily, but you should know which arrangement you have bought. Some providers style the table and leave; others remain to restyle and monitor. Both are common. What causes problems is assuming someone is present who is not, particularly across a long service window.

What should I tell guests with serious allergies?

Tell them exactly what the provider told you, including the limits. A shared grazing surface is difficult to guarantee, and an honest description lets the guest decide for themselves. If a guest's needs are serious, ask the provider whether a separately prepared plated option is possible.
