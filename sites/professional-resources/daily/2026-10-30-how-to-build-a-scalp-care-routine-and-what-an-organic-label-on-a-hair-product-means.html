How to Build a Scalp Care Routine, and What an Organic Label on a Hair Product Means

Short answer: Match the wash schedule to how quickly your scalp gets oily, put shampoo on the scalp rather than the full length, condition after every wash, and handle hair gently while it is wet. The American Academy of Dermatology suggests shampooing dry, textured, curly or thick hair when needed and at least every 2 to 3 weeks, and its guidance for Black hair suggests once a week or every other week so product does not build up. On labels, organic is a USDA-defined claim, not an FDA safety standard.

Start with how fast your scalp gets oily, not with a product

A scalp routine is mostly a schedule. The American Academy of Dermatology, in its guidance titled Tips for healthy hair, says to wash hair based on how often it gets dirty or oily: someone with straight hair and an oily scalp may want to shampoo every day, while dry, textured, curly or thick hair can be shampooed when needed, at least once every 2 to 3 weeks.

The Academy's separate page on everyday care for Black hair is more specific for tightly coiled hair: wash once a week or every other week, which helps keep hair-care products from building up, and build-up can be drying to the hair. Those two pieces of guidance agree on the principle. The right interval is the shortest one that keeps the scalp clean without leaving the hair dry.

Write the interval down for a month. Most people who say their scalp is never comfortable have never kept the wash day fixed long enough to see what the scalp does on day three versus day ten.

A four-week scalp routine log

Keeping a short record turns a vague sense that the scalp is never right into something you can change one variable at a time.

  1. Pick a wash day and keep it fixed for four weeks.
  2. Note how the scalp feels on day two, day five and the day before the next wash: oily, tight, itchy or comfortable.
  3. Put shampoo on the scalp only, and conditioner on the ends or the full length depending on hair type.
  4. Write down every styling product used and how long it stays in.
  5. Note any style that felt tight or painful while it was being done.
  6. At the end of four weeks, move the wash day earlier or later by a few days if the log shows a pattern.
  7. Book a board-certified dermatologist for anything that hurts, spreads, or changes the look of the hair.

Shampoo is for the scalp; conditioner is for the length

The same Academy guidance says to apply shampoo to the scalp instead of the entire length of the hair. That cleanses away built-up product, dead skin and excess oil where they collect, without drying the length more than necessary.

Conditioner follows every wash. The Academy says conditioner moisturizes and detangles hair and makes it easier to manage; fine or straight hair needs it on the ends, while dry or curly hair needs it along the full length. Its page on Black hair adds that the ends are the oldest and most fragile part of the hair and should always be coated.

Gentle handling is part of the routine, not an extra

Hair is delicate when it is wet. The Academy recommends a wide-tooth comb rather than a brush for detangling wet hair, combing thick curly hair in the shower before the conditioner is rinsed out, and blotting with a towel or t-shirt rather than rubbing hair dry.

Tension matters as much as product. For braids, cornrows and weaves, the Academy's advice is plain: if it hurts while the hair is being styled, ask the stylist to stop and redo it, because pain equals damage. Its list of hair-care habits that can damage hair also includes pulling hair back tightly and using styling products with long-lasting hold, and suggests looser styles that do not pull.

Reading a hair product label, including the word organic

Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, as the FDA explains on its page Is It a Cosmetic, a Drug, or Both?, a cosmetic is a product applied for cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness or altering appearance. Cleansing shampoos are named in that definition.

A product whose intended use is to affect the structure or function of the body, or to address a disease, is a drug, and the FDA notes that claims alone can make a product a drug. That is a useful reading test for any scalp product: words about cleansing, conditioning, softening and removing residue describe a cosmetic. Promises about the body itself belong to a different category with different rules, and they are a reason to look more carefully rather than less.

Organic is the label word shoppers ask about most. The FDA's page on organic cosmetics says the FDA has no definition of organic for cosmetics; the term is defined and certified under the U.S. Department of Agriculture's National Organic Program, and a cosmetic labeled organic must meet both the USDA's rules for that claim and the FDA's cosmetic rules. The same page says an ingredient's source does not determine its safety, and that many plants contain substances that can be irritating or allergenic, so a botanical or organic product still deserves a patch test.

When a routine is not the answer

Flakes are common and often routine-related: the Academy lists not shampooing often enough, or not using the right conditioner, oil or scalp moisturizer for the hair type, as common reasons for them. A change of schedule is a reasonable first step.

Persistent itching, tenderness, redness, patches, or any change in the texture or appearance of the hair are different. The Academy advises seeing a board-certified dermatologist when hair changes, and says that even slight noticeable thinning is worth having looked at early. No shampoo, conditioner or oil is a substitute for that visit.

Frequently asked questions

How often should textured or curly hair be washed?

The American Academy of Dermatology suggests shampooing dry, textured, curly or thick hair when needed, at least once every 2 to 3 weeks, and its guidance for Black hair suggests once a week or every other week to keep product from building up.

Should shampoo go on the whole head of hair?

The Academy recommends applying shampoo to the scalp rather than the entire length, which cleanses where oil and product collect without drying the rest of the hair more than necessary.

Is conditioner needed every time?

Yes, according to the Academy: conditioner after every wash moisturizes and detangles, and the ends, as the oldest part of the hair, should always be coated.

Does an organic label mean a hair product is safer?

No. The FDA says an ingredient's source does not determine its safety, and that organic is defined by the USDA's National Organic Program rather than by the FDA.

When should a scalp problem go to a dermatologist?

When it persists after a change in routine, hurts, spreads, or comes with any change in the texture or appearance of the hair. The Academy recommends a board-certified dermatologist for those.
