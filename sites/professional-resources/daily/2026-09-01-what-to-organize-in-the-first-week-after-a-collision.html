What to Organize in the First Week After a Collision

Short answer: In the first week, collect and date six things: the police or incident report reference, photographs of the scene and vehicles, contact details for everyone involved and any witnesses, the insurance correspondence, all medical records including the initial visit, and a dated note of what you remember. Records are easiest to obtain early and hardest to reconstruct later.

What this page recommends

In the first week, collect and date six things: the police or incident report reference, photographs of the scene and vehicles, contact details for everyone involved and any witnesses, the insurance correspondence, all medical records including the initial visit, and a dated note of what you remember.

Why the first week matters for records specifically

Records degrade. Photographs of a scene are only possible in the first hours, witness memories fade, and businesses with security footage frequently overwrite it within days. None of this is about building a case; it is about the fact that a record which exists now may not exist in a month.

The other reason is that early records are dated. A note written in week 1 carries different weight from the same account reconstructed in month 6, and that difference does not depend on anyone's honesty.

The first-week list

Do these in order. The first three are time-sensitive in a way the rest are not.

  1. Note the report reference and which agency holds it.
  2. Ask nearby businesses or residences whether they have footage, and ask in writing.
  3. Write down witness contact details before they become unreachable.
  4. Photograph vehicles before any repair or disposal.
  5. Attend medical appointments and keep every record and referral.
  6. Write a dated account of what you remember within 7 days, and do not revise it later.
  7. Keep every piece of insurance correspondence, and note every call.
  8. Start a single dated folder and back it up.
  9. Speak with a licensed attorney before giving a recorded statement or signing anything.
  10. Ask that attorney what deadlines apply in your state.

What to collect, and how to keep it

Start with the official record: the report reference number, the agency that took it, and how to obtain a copy. Then photographs, of vehicles, the scene, road conditions, and any visible injury, with the date preserved in the file rather than only in your memory.

Collect contact details for every party and every witness, including anyone who stopped and left. Witness details are the item most often lost, because it feels awkward to ask at the time and is impossible afterwards.

Keep all medical records, including the first visit even if it seemed minor, plus referrals, prescriptions, and appointment records. Keep every piece of insurance correspondence in full, and note the date and content of any phone call.

Store all of it in one place, in date order, and back it up. A single folder in date order is worth more than a more elaborate system that nobody maintains.

Two cautions

Be careful with recorded statements and with signing anything before you understand it. Whether and when to give a statement, and what a release or settlement document does, are legal questions that depend on your state. A licensed attorney can answer them; a general article cannot.

Be equally careful with social media. Posts, photographs, and check-ins made after an incident routinely become part of the record, and the account is not always the one the poster intended to give.

Health questions belong with a clinician. Attend follow-up appointments and describe symptoms accurately to the people treating you. Records of treatment are the by-product of getting care, not a reason to seek it.

Frequently asked questions

How soon should I speak with an attorney?

Sooner rather than later, because deadlines and the effect of early statements vary by state and some are shorter than people expect. An initial consultation is often available before any commitment. What a general article cannot tell you is which deadlines apply to you; a licensed attorney in your state can.

Should I give a recorded statement to an insurer?

That is a question to put to a licensed attorney before agreeing, not after. Whether to give one, when, and in what form depends on your circumstances and your jurisdiction, and a recorded statement given early can be difficult to revisit later.

What if I did not feel injured at the time?

Symptoms that appear later are a medical question, and the right response is to see a clinician and describe accurately what you are experiencing. Keep the records from every visit, including the first, since the timing of assessment and treatment is part of the record regardless of how you felt initially.

How should the records be organized?

One folder, in date order, backed up in a second location. Elaborate systems are abandoned; a dated folder is not. When you later hand material to a professional, a chronological set with the source of each item noted saves time you would otherwise pay for.
